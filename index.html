01

A shared platform for fifteen industrial sites

AIM Recycling · Montréal · 2023–2024 · Infrastructure Architect & Project Manager
Plants can't stop productionRegulatory reporting must not break15 sitesSite-wave rollout

The problem

Each plant ran its own spreadsheets, databases and equipment telemetry. Leadership could not compare sites, and every change risked interrupting production or regulatory reporting.

How I framed it

I built the business case and a site-wave roadmap with plant managers and finance: baseline, target outcomes and a budget per wave. I scheduled each cut-over around production plans with operations.

What shipped

  • One documented data model on AWS replacing site-specific tools
  • Event-driven ingestion of equipment data (Kafka, MQTT, Kinesis) into S3
  • Site-by-site schedules and status views shared by leadership and plant managers

Working with users

Change management with site managers and staff: procedures, training and adoption tracking for every wave, so plant teams could run the platform on their own.

After launchLessons from each wave went into the next wave's templates, so later sites reused the improved process and avoided earlier issues. Migrations never interrupted plant activity or regulatory reporting.
02

Hospital systems that fit around clinical calendars

Montreal Heart Institute · 2024–2025 · Cloud Architecture Consultant & Project Manager
Clinical downtime windows onlyPHIPA & Quebec Law 25HL7 FHIRBilingual

The problem

The hospital needed new Azure application, identity and data-protection capabilities without disrupting care, and every release had to satisfy privacy officers as well as clinicians.

How I framed it

I set deliverables and acceptance criteria with department heads and the IT committee, presenting options, risks and costs in French and English before each decision.

What shipped

  • Releases timed to clinical downtime windows
  • HL7 FHIR interfaces and Azure services, accepted against security evidence
  • Access controls, encryption and data classification checked before go-live

Working with users

I adapted technical material for clinicians, researchers, privacy officers and executives, and documented decisions and follow-ups after every meeting.

After launchWe tracked data availability, data quality, integration reliability and user adoption, and reported each indicator with its owner. Bilingual runbooks, training and a closure report let the internal team run the systems safely afterwards.
03

One platform standard for forty development squads

Desjardins · 2024 · Program Manager, Multi-Squad Delivery
Squads move at different speeds40+ internal teamsOpen Banking APIs

The problem

More than forty squads needed a secure CI/CD platform and the Open Banking API roadmap, but each team had its own priorities and could not wait for a central team.

How I framed it

I treated the squads as internal customers: platform work was prioritized by value and dependency, and each release was tied to outcomes for advisors and fintech partners.

What shipped

  • A delivery roadmap for the Open Banking API platform
  • Reference patterns, onboarding guides and runbooks
  • A dependency board and remediation burn-down leadership could read at a glance

Working with users

Bilingual adoption workshops and regular follow-ups with squad leads, with documented actions after each session.

After launchSquads adopted one platform standard without waiting on the central team. Vendor performance and outstanding exposure were reviewed with leadership at every increment.
04

Partner integrations people could actually build against

Global Payments · 2021–2022 · DevOps Integrator & Technical Project Lead
PCI DSS change controlSettlement cycles fixedMerchant-facing APIs

The problem

Third-party and merchant integrations kept failing on inconsistent requirements, while every change had to stay traceable for payment-industry assessors.

How I framed it

I turned partner requirements into integration scope, API specifications and acceptance criteria, with consistent authentication, error handling and message formats.

What shipped

  • Specifications and test conditions shared with each partner
  • Release-readiness and change-advisory sessions with clear owners
  • Traceable records for every certificate, WAF and network change

Working with users

Release schedules and status reports let product, compliance and operations follow every cycle. I escalated early, with options, around merchant commitments.

After launchI documented the causes of partner connectivity failures for operations and handed over evidence procedures, so audit evidence could be produced quickly after each project closed.
05

Marketplace features ranked by what partners reported

Sherweb · 2019–2020 · Cloud Engineer & Technical Project Lead
Multi-tenant isolationManaged-service providers across North AmericaMicrosoft 365

The problem

A cloud marketplace used by managed-service providers needed new features and Azure environments without weakening tenant data separation.

How I framed it

With product owners I defined scope and acceptance criteria so each feature delivered measurable value to partners and could be tested objectively.

What shipped

  • Partner-platform features and Azure environment builds
  • Release notes and bilingual summaries for business stakeholders
  • Features checked against ISO 27001 and GDPR before release

Working with users

Design reviews, Scrum ceremonies and release-readiness meetings, plus direct follow-up with Microsoft partner contacts on partner-facing issues.

After launchI proposed fixes from support and telemetry data, so the problems partners reported most often were fixed first and did not return in later releases.
